## 1. PPWR, les trois erreurs

> **Three things printers still get wrong about PPWR recyclability.**
>
> The regulation applies on 12 August. Here is what we keep hearing in pressrooms, and what Regulation (EU) 2025/40 actually says.
>
> ▪ "Recyclability is a 2030 problem." It is not. Article 6(1) applies from 12 August 2026. From that date, all packaging placed on the EU market must be recyclable.
> ▪ "We are aiming for grade D." There is no grade D. The grades are A, B and C. Below C, packaging is described as technically non-recyclable and carries no letter at all.

>
> The graded thresholds come later: at least grade C from 1 January 2030, or 24 months after the relevant delegated acts, whichever is later. At least grade B from 1 January 2038.
>

> **The PPWR applies on 12 August. This is not only a European deadline.**
>
> If your packaging is placed on the EU market, it does not matter where you print it. From that date, every packaging type needs a signed Declaration of Conformity, backed by technical documentation.
>
> What that means in practice for a printer:
>
> ▪ Know which packaging types you produce and who places them on the market
> ▪ Have the technical file that supports the declaration, not just the declaration
> ▪ Be able to show the production data behind it, measured rather than asserted
>
> The part printers underestimate: a compliance file is only as solid as the production records behind it. Data that was measured and logged is far easier to defend than data reconstructed after the fact.
>
> Honest question for the packaging printers here: are your clients asking you for this yet, or is it still silent on their side?

## 5. Gamme étendue

**Image :** `Assets for Website Rutherford.fr/Images : Photos/Produtcs printing packagin/Luxurypackagingboxparfum.jpg`
Un packaging luxe, saturé, avec des couleurs que le CMJN seul tient mal. Le sujet

> **Extended gamut is sold as a color capability. It is really a production economics decision.**
>
> Seven inks instead of four plus spot colors. The pitch is always the same: fewer washups, fewer ink changes, more jobs through the same press.

> All true. But it only pays above a certain job mix.
>

>
> ▪ Not in the color, in the ink changes you stop making

> **The element most likely to get a packaging job rejected is not the image. It is the flat area of the brand color.**
>
> The eye forgives a picture. It does not forgive a large flat solid, especially one the client recognises from across a room.

> Why spot colors are harder to hold than process colors:
>
> ▪ A flat area gives the eye a reference to compare against, a photograph does not

> ▪ The tolerance in the contract is usually tighter than for process
>
> Which is why agreeing to a spot color tolerance without knowing what your press can actually hold over a run is a bad idea. Agree to a number you can measure and repeat, not one that sounds impressive in a meeting.

## 10. G7 vs ISO 12647

**Image :** carte texte en deux colonnes, « ISO 12647 » à gauche, « G7 » à droite,
trois lignes sous chacune. Sur un sujet de normes, un visuel de comparaison est
lu, une photo est ignorée.
*Alternative photo :* `Downloads/Photo for blog rutherford.fr/DSC_3810.jpg`
(opérateur qui annote une plaque devant le râtelier, le côté artisanal du métier).

> **G7 or ISO 12647? The question comes up in every color audit, and it is usually the wrong question.**
>
> They are not rivals. They are two routes to the same destination, predictable and repeatable color.
>
> ▪ ISO 12647 controls the printing condition itself, ink, substrate, densities, dot gain, the target values the press should hit
> ▪ G7 controls the visual appearance of the gray scale, so that a job looks the same across different processes even when the underlying numbers differ
>
> Which means many printers do not have to choose. ISO 12647 for the press condition, G7 for cross-process appearance, is a perfectly coherent combination.
>
> What neither of them does is hold the condition once the press has been running for six hours. That is a control problem rather than a standards problem.
>
> Which one does your shop actually run, and did you choose it or did a client choose it for you?

# Direction artistique : ce qui marche pour ces posts

## Les cinq règles

**1. De vraies photos d'atelier, jamais d'IA.** Le public est composé
d'imprimeurs. Ils repèrent une image générée en une seconde, et à partir de là ils
ne croient plus le texte non plus. La banque contient assez de vraies photos pour
tenir des mois.

**2. Une machine identifiable.** Un logo Manroland, un capot Heidelberg, un écran
de console : l'opérateur reconnaît son quotidien et s'arrête. Une photo générique
d'usine ne déclenche rien.

**3. Des gens, dès que le sujet le permet.** Les posts avec des visages font
systématiquement plus de commentaires que les posts avec des machines seules.
Post 9 est l'exemple type.

**4. Format 1200 x 627 px minimum, en paysage.** Les photos verticales sont
rognées violemment sur mobile. Quand la seule bonne image est verticale, la
recadrer en 4:5 plutôt qu'en 16:9, LinkedIn laisse plus de hauteur en 4:5.

**5. Pas de texte incrusté sur les photos.** Sauf pour les cartes texte
volontaires (posts 2 et 10), où le visuel EST le message.

## Les carrousels

Le format carrousel (PDF multipage) reste celui qui génère le plus de temps passé
sur un post LinkedIn. Deux des dix posts s'y prêtent naturellement :

- **Post 4** : une marque de presse par slide, avec le nom de la console
- **Post 1** : une erreur PPWR par slide, la correction en dessous

Si tu ne dois en produire qu'un seul, fais le post 4.
