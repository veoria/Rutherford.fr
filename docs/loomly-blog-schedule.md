## 1. Mardi 11 août : Gamme étendue

> Extended gamut is sold as a color capability. It is really a production economics decision.
>
> Seven inks instead of four plus spot colors. Fewer washups, fewer ink changes, more jobs through the same press.
>
> All true. But it only pays above a certain job mix.
>
> ▪ The money is not in the color, it is in the ink changes you stop making
> ▪ Short runs with many job changes benefit most
> ▪ Long runs on few spot colors benefit least
> ▪ A six-unit press changes the arithmetic considerably
>
> And the prerequisite nobody prices in: extended gamut only works if the press holds its color. Seven channels drifting is worse than four, because every brand color is now built from a combination rather than mixed in a can.

## 3. Mardi 25 août : Couleurs de marque

> The element most likely to get a packaging job rejected is not the image. It is the flat area of the brand color.
>
> The eye forgives a picture. It does not forgive a large flat solid, especially one the client recognises from across a room.
>
> Why spot colors are harder to hold than process colors:
>
> ▪ A flat area gives the eye a reference to compare against, a photograph does not
> ▪ There is no other channel to hide behind, the color is the color
> ▪ Ink film thickness variation shows directly, with nothing to mask it
> ▪ The tolerance in the contract is usually tighter than for process
>
> Which is why agreeing to a spot color tolerance without knowing what your press can actually hold over a run is a bad idea. Agree to a number you can measure and repeat, not one that sounds impressive in a meeting.
>

## 5. Mardi 8 septembre : PPWR, les trois erreurs

> Three things printers still get wrong about PPWR recyclability.
>
> The regulation has been applying since 12 August. Here is what we keep hearing in pressrooms, and what Regulation (EU) 2025/40 actually says.
>
> ▪ "Recyclability is a 2030 problem." It is not. Article 6(1) applies from 12 August 2026. All packaging placed on the EU market must be recyclable.
> ▪ "We are aiming for grade D." There is no grade D. The grades are A, B and C. Below C, packaging is described as technically non-recyclable and carries no letter at all.
> ▪ "EN 18120 covers us." It is published, but it is not cited in the Official Journal. Conforming to it gives you no presumption of conformity today.
>
> The graded thresholds come later: at least grade C from 1 January 2030, or 24 months after the relevant delegated acts, whichever is later. At least grade B from 1 January 2038.
>

## 7. Mardi 22 septembre : Multi-sites

> The same carton, printed in France, in the United States and in Asia. It has to look identical on shelf.
>
> Different presses, different inks, different paper, different operators. Same brand color.
>
> That is not achieved by sending everyone the same PDF. What a multi-site standard actually requires:
>
> ▪ One reference, defined in measured values rather than in a printed sample
> ▪ The same measurement conditions in every plant, M0 to M3 matters here
> ▪ A tolerance every site can actually hold, not the tightest one on the list
> ▪ A way to prove compliance per job, not per audit
>
> Closed loop is what makes the standard portable. The reference travels as data, and every press corrects toward it in real time, whatever brand of press it is.

## 8. Mardi 29 septembre : Automatisation

> Every 2026 print survey says the same thing: pressroom automation is no longer a differentiator. It is the baseline.
>
> Plate changing, ink presetting, inline inspection. Most offset shops have all three. Yet very few describe their production as highly optimized.
>
> The gap is almost always in the same place. On the press, the last manual loop is color.
>
> And here is the uncomfortable part: automation without color consistency does not fix drift. It just produces the drift faster.
>
> To be clear about what closes that loop. The system measures the sheet and corrects the ink keys. It does not set up a press, it does not read a difficult substrate, and it does not decide anything. The judgement stays with the printer. What changes is that the printer stops spending the shift on the one task a machine genuinely does better.

## 9. Mardi 6 octobre : PPWR, ce qui a changé

> The PPWR has been applying since 12 August. This was never only a European deadline.
>
> If your packaging is placed on the EU market, it does not matter where you print it. Every packaging type needs a signed Declaration of Conformity, backed by technical documentation.
>
> What that means in practice for a printer:
>
> ▪ Know which packaging types you produce and who places them on the market
> ▪ Have the technical file that supports the declaration, not just the declaration
> ▪ Be able to show the production data behind it, measured rather than asserted
>
> The part printers underestimate: a compliance file is only as solid as the production records behind it. Data that was measured and logged is far easier to defend than data reconstructed after the fact.

## 10. Mardi 13 octobre : G7 vs ISO 12647

> G7 or ISO 12647? The question comes up in every color audit, and it is usually the wrong question.
>
> They are not rivals. They are two routes to the same destination, predictable and repeatable color.
>
> ▪ ISO 12647 controls the printing condition itself, ink, substrate, densities, dot gain, the target values the press should hit
> ▪ G7 controls the visual appearance of the gray scale, so that a job looks the same across different processes even when the underlying numbers differ
>
> Which means many printers do not have to choose. ISO 12647 for the press condition, G7 for cross-process appearance, is a perfectly coherent combination.
>
> What neither of them does is hold the condition once the press has been running for six hours. That is a control problem rather than a standards problem.
>
